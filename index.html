John Locke Essay Competition

The Asymmetry of Moral Obligations

We can ground our obligations to the living in ordinary morality. Our obligations to future people cannot be grounded that way at all.

Moral disagreement is permanent, and we cannot know how future values will diverge from ours, so fixed precepts are the wrong tool for questions like climate change. Using Moehler’s multilevel social contract theory, the essay separates intuitive first-level morality from second-level bargaining among instrumentally rational agents, and argues that only the second can bind us across generations.

Very High Commendation · top 1% globally

II

Harvard International Economics Essay Contest

Beyond Carbon Subsidies

Green subsidies buy almost no abatement at enormous cost. Carbon taxes buy a great deal at a fraction of it, and are still the harder policy to keep.

U.S. renewable subsidies cut CO₂ by roughly 0.3% a year; global biofuel subsidies raised emissions by 4.8 million tons. Britain’s carbon price cut power-sector emissions 55%. Abatement costs tell the same story — €206 and €978 a ton for German wind and solar against €36 and €55 in Britain. But repeals and fuel-price revolts show the efficient instrument is the fragile one.

Independent Research

Why One Child?

The one-child policy is usually read as an authoritarian state stopping at nothing. It is better read as a state that had lost faith in ideology and put its faith in science instead.

After Mao’s campaigns the Party rejected the ideological wholesale, and Scientism filled the vacuum: the Song group’s control-theory models carried an authority social scientists could not match, reinforced by Western overpopulation anxiety from The Population Bomb and Limits to Growth. What followed was a shrinking workforce, a skewed sex ratio, and rapid aging.

IV

High School Fed Challenge, FRB New York

Lett-uce Unpack

Fast food is not just a consumer market. It is the channel through which agricultural consolidation reaches the labor market and the price level.

A podcast script for the New York Fed’s competition, tracing the line from concentrated animal feeding operations and agricultural monopolization through volatility in food-service employment to consumer prices, then proposing tax incentives for food deserts and community nutrition education.

Published in the Journal of Future Economics · 12 of 195

Evaluating Governmental Controls on Social Media Speech

Horizon Academic Essay Prize, 2025

Honorable Mention · top 50 of 1,952 international entries

A competition judged by faculty from Yale, Harvard, Oxford, and Cambridge. The essay argues that state regulation of disinformation fails on its own terms: Germany’s 2017 Network Enforcement Act pushed platforms to over-block rather than adjudicate, and cut disinformation very little; AI takedowns produce enormous numbers of false positives and have almost no multilingual training data; and compliance-driven moderation moves slower than the misinformation it chases. Independent fact-checking and media literacy are the more durable alternative, because they do not require the state to decide what is true.

ii

The Meritocracy Dilemma

CCIR Re:Think Competition, 2026

Honorable Mention

Meritocracy is a myth — not in the sense that it disguises structural inequality, but in the stricter sense that it cannot satisfy its own justificatory conditions. The essay takes on desert-based meritocracy, the claim that allocations are intrinsically justified because people deserve outcomes according to their merits, and argues it is incompatible with the liberal societies that are the only place desert is intelligible as a fairness criterion in the first place. Set out the conditions that would justify it, and the conditions turn out to be ones liberalism cannot meet.

iii

Against Moral Intuitionism

John Locke Essay Competition, Philosophy, 2024; The Sidgwick Prize, Cambridge, Philosophy, 2026

High Commendation and Shortlist respectively

We have no reason to trust our moral intuitions. Taking intuition in the foundationalist sense — a self-evident “intellectual seeming” — the essay argues they are epistemically unreliable, subject to persistent disagreement, and resting on a questionable metaphysics. The central asymmetry is with perception: a perceptual seeming can be checked against contextual cues, so we know to distrust it in fog or when impaired. An intellectual seeming admits no such feedback. And because moral intuitionism presupposes moral realism, every argument against the latter counts against it too.

iv

The Zhejiang-Jiangxi Campaign

Independent research

The Doolittle raid of April 1942 did little physical damage and a great deal for American morale, and that is where the story usually stops. It leaves out what the Japanese did next: a retaliatory campaign through Zhejiang and Jiangxi in which roughly 250,000 civilians were killed, property was destroyed wholesale, and biological weapons were used indiscriminately. The paper traces Sino-American cooperation from the isolationism of the mid-1930s through the shift in public opinion after the Marco Polo Bridge Incident, and argues that the raid’s consequences have to be reassessed against the devastation that followed it.
